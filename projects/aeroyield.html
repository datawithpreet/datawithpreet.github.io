04 · Aviation · Revenue Management · Agentic AI

A $7 Profit per Passenger. One Bad Forecast Can Change the Economics of a Flight.

Airlines must price perishable inventory in a market that never stands still. AeroYield AI combines demand forecasts, competitor fares, major events, fuel costs, and market signals to recommend pricing and inventory decisions that support revenue and profitability — always staged for human approval, never written straight to a booking system.

Real-world proof: IATA reports the global aviation profit margin is razor thin — just 3.7%, or a little above $7 per passenger per segment — which is why pricing and revenue management are, in IATA's words, "the heartbeat of aviation."
IATA: Revenue Management, the Heartbeat of Aviation ↗

The business outcome

Pricing mistakes are expensive in both directions: price too high and seats fly empty; price too low and you sell out cheap. The outcome this project demonstrates: an agent that runs the Observe → Analyze → Decide → Act → Verify loop a revenue manager runs — on incoming signals, out loud, with transparent arithmetic — and prices for profit, not just revenue. The centerpiece is a three-way baseline comparison (fixed tariffs vs. rule-based pricing vs. agentic pricing) on the same simulated scenarios, so you can see exactly what the agent's decisions buy you, fare by fare: agentic profit $30,050 vs fixed $24,380 (+23%), 110 seats sold vs 92, forecast error 25% vs 48%.

Positioned as airline revenue intelligence: passenger-airline pricing and seat inventory — not cargo freight forecasting.

How the agent works

Setup: one airline, one route (SFO → BOS), 180 seats, current fare $320, 120 booked / 60 remaining, 14 days to departure. A chatbot could describe revenue management. This system runs the decision loop, out loud, on incoming signals:

1
Observe

Three intelligence tools scan demand (festival announced, booking pace +7% ahead of baseline), competitors (comparability-verified fares), and market/cost signals (fuel +12%, confidence steady).

2
Analyze

The pricing decision engine evaluates candidate fares on transparent arithmetic: demand forecast D(p), expected revenue R(p), contribution (p − cost) × sold, capacity-capped at 60 seats.

3
Decide

Recommends $320 → $350: explains which signals fired, why $350 beats $280/$320/$360/$400, what could invalidate it, and a sensitivity check (±20% demand → still $350).

4
Act

The move is staged to an approval queue (simulation); a human approves. The agent never writes to a live booking system.

5
Verify

Sets up what to check next: 72h pace vs forecast, competitor response, auto-flag if pace falls below 80% of forecast.

6
The competitor-drop twist

When a competitor drops to $270, the agent verifies the fare is comparable — then does NOT auto-follow. It recommends a fenced $299 fare class (20 seats) while holding $350: best contribution, no price war. That's what a revenue manager does — and what a chatbot cannot.

The counterintuitive lesson the demo teaches on the baseline curve: $280 beats $400 on expected revenue ($48,200 vs $44,000) because volume wins when demand is elastic — exactly the kind of thing gut feel gets wrong.

Data engineering

All inputs are synthetic and hand-built: demand curves D(p) for baseline, festival, competitor-$270, and soft-market scenarios (hand-built anchors, linearly interpolated); signal set — Boston Harbor Music Festival announced (+24 must-fly pax, confidence 0.78), booking pace +7% ahead, competitor fares $340/$335 (verified comparable), jet fuel +12% ($55 → ~$58 cost/pax); decision constants — $750 change threshold, candidate fares [$280…$400].

External signals — holidays, events, competitor pricing, fuel-cost changes — influence forecasts and recommendations; they do not reliably predict an exact fare on their own. The demo prints an honest caveat under the comparison.

Architecture

LayerWhat it does
Intelligence toolsThree signal scanners: demand intelligence, competitor intelligence (with comparability verification), market/cost intelligence.
Pricing decision engineEvaluates candidate fares on D(p), R(p), and contribution arithmetic — fully transparent, no black box.
Agent loopObserve → Analyze → Decide → Act → Verify, printed as a styled trace in the web UI.
Approval queueEvery pricing move is staged for human approval; nothing executes autonomously (simulated).
Baseline comparatorFixed vs. rule-based vs. agentic strategies on identical scenarios: revenue, profit, seats sold, unsold, forecast error, volatility, shock-scenario performance.
Web UIapp.py serves the demo over stdlib http.server; runs demo.py via subprocess and renders the run as a styled agent trace.

Implementation

  • Deterministic: stdlib only, fully offline, no API keys — the demo finishes in well under a second.
  • Live scenario: three events hit at once, the competitor-drop twist, four scenario simulations, and the three-way strategy comparison — all in simulation, all explained.
  • Headline numbers: agentic profit $30,050 vs fixed $24,380 (+23%); 110 seats sold vs 92; forecast error 25% vs 48%; shock-scenario profit $10,360 vs $5,300.
  • Guardrail: unusual market conditions are flagged for analyst approval; the agent explains what could invalidate each recommendation.

Technical details

  • Run it: python3 app.py, then open http://localhost:8004 in Chrome and click Run live demo (binds 127.0.0.1, port 8004).
  • Repo: ynpreet/aeroyield-ai ↗
  • Companion project: Flight fare prediction web app (deployed ML) ↗
  • Success criteria: the three-way comparison prints revenue, profit, seats sold/unsold, forecast error, volatility, and shock-scenario performance with the honest caveat; the competitor-drop twist stages a fenced fare class instead of auto-following.
