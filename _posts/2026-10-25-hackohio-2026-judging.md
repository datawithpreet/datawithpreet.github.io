Thank you to the HackOHI/O team at Ohio State for the opportunity to judge at HackOHI/O 2026.

## What HackOHI/O is, and why it matters

HackOHI/O is Ohio State University's flagship hackathon, and Ohio's largest hackathon. It started in 2013 with just 100 students coding in the basement of Thompson Library, founded to bring a "hacker culture" of coding and building to Columbus. Since then it has grown, year after year, to more than 800 participants filling the Ohio Union Grand Ballroom, with spin-off events including MakeOHI/O, High School I/O, and more. It celebrated its 10th anniversary in 2022, and it is now a student-run programme that supports nearly a dozen competitive events, plus workshops, project grants, and scholarships.

That history is why judging there matters. This is not a one-off campus event. It is a programme that has, for over a decade, been the place where Ohio State students — from computer science to finance, psychology, data analytics, and even violin performance — learn by doing: form a team of two to four, pick an idea or a sponsor challenge, build a working prototype in about 24 hours, and present it to judges who include students, faculty, and industry professionals. No experience is required to take part, and students are supported by volunteer mentors throughout. For many of them, it is their first time building something real, outside a classroom, with a deadline that does not move.

I am recording the placement exactly as the organisers recorded it. I am not claiming a selection process I have not seen documented — what is documented is the assignment itself, in Room 2, across two waves, and that is what this post records.

## What I did, exactly

I judged eight teams in Room 2. Judging, in this event's tradition, is about technical difficulty, creativity, usefulness, and presentation — but in practice, what I actually did was watch each pitch, review the team's work, and ask what actually held up: does it work, who is it for, what did you build yourself in the time you had, and what would break first if you kept going?

The students I judged were Ohio State students working in small teams of two to four, building in a single weekend. That is a very different kind of work from a semester project: it rewards clear scope, a demo that runs, and honest limits, and it punishes scope that is too big to finish. My job was to tell that difference, team by team, fairly, and to do it the same way for the first team in Wave 1 and the last team in Wave 2.

What stood out across all eight, as across DS+X the same weekend, was the same thing: students building for a real user they could name, in a weekend, with a demo they had to make work in front of judges.

## My experience of the event

Judging at HackOHI/O, in the same weekend as DS+X in Boston, gave me a useful comparison. Two different universities, two different student cultures, the same lesson: the best student work is not the most complicated work. It is the work where the team can explain, in plain words, what problem they chose, what they actually built, and what they would do next if they had another week. That is what I looked for in Room 2, and that is what I will keep looking for as a judge.

Thank you to Elizabeth Stong and the HackOHI/O organising team for running the event and for keeping volunteers in the loop. I would love to collaborate again in upcoming events.
