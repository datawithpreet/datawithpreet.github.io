## What PyData is, and why it matters

PyData is a community-driven gathering of users and developers of data analysis tools in Python. It began with the first PyData Workshop in March 2012, in Silicon Valley, and grew into a global community: PyData New York later in 2012, PyData Boston in July 2013, and PyData conferences and meetups around the world since. Its goal, from the start, has been simple and practical: give Python enthusiasts a place to share ideas and learn from each other about how best to apply the language and its tools to real data problems — data management, processing, analytics, and visualisation — with tutorials for novices, workshops for practitioners, and a place for package developers and users to meet in person. Proceeds support NumFOCUS and the open-source tools data scientists actually use.

That history matters for a talk like mine. PyData is not a vendor conference. It is a practitioner community: people who clean messy data, build pipelines, and explain models to people who have to act on them. A talk there is judged, in the room, on whether it helps someone do their next piece of work better — not on how impressive the slides look.

## What my talk is about, exactly

- **Topic:** Data Design Practices for EHR Data
- **Time:** 7:10 PM – 7:55 PM, Wednesday, November 4, 2026
- **Event:** PyData Boston, Nov 4, 2026, 6:30 PM

Electronic health record data is some of the messiest, most consequential data a data engineer will ever touch. It comes from many systems, in many formats, with missing values that mean different things in different places, codes that change over time, and patients behind every row. My talk is about the design practices that make that data trustworthy enough to use: how you model it, how you clean it without silently changing what it means, how you document its limits, and how you build pipelines that a care team, a researcher, or a clinician can actually rely on — not just a demo that works once.

That is the work I do every day in healthcare analytics: architecture, pipelines, data governance, and making sure that when a model or a dashboard says something, someone can trace it back to the data it came from. This talk is that work, explained for practitioners who do the same kind of work in Python.

## Who the audience is

The audience is the PyData Boston community: data scientists, data engineers, analysts, and developers who work with Python and with real data, at all levels. I am not writing this talk for experts only, and I am not writing it as a beginner tutorial either. I am writing it for people who have felt the same problem — data that looks clean until you try to use it — and want practices they can take back to their own teams the next day.
